### What follows from these tables

1. **The threshold changed the plan.** `BHJ` rises 1→2→3→4 and `SMJ` falls 3→2→1→0 across
   the sweep. A flat time curve here cannot be attributed to the optimizer ignoring the
   setting.
2. **The default already broadcasts everything.** `default-10MiB`, `admits-4` and
   `above-all-8x` agree to within 0.3 s in every query and carry identical operator counts.
   Raising the threshold beyond the default changes nothing at this scale.
3. **On Q2 the relational plan is faster than the pattern**: 12.21 s against 17.37 s, a
   ratio of 0.70×. This **confirms** the manuscript, which already reports the pattern as
   1.67× slower than BHJ on Q2 (`joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex:1638-1639`). See §5.1.
4. **The pattern's advantage is largest where broadcasting is unavailable.** Q1 at
   `admits-1` costs 134.78 s against the pattern's 19.85 s (6.79×). Against a
   fully-broadcast plan the same query gives 1.36×.
5. **Broadcast is paid per query.** Q1 spends 4.6–5.0 s of every relational run on broadcast
   exchanges. The pattern's equivalent — Phase 1 — cost 3.2–3.7 s and, unlike the exchange,
   is not rebuilt by a second query in the same session. This benchmark runs one query per
   JVM and so cannot show that; it is measured separately in §10.

### What follows from these tables

1. **At 100 MB the default threshold stops broadcasting the dimension.** `customer`
   estimates at 66,777,534 B, above the 10 MiB default, so the default configuration falls
   to `BHJ=3, SMJ=1` and costs 56.26 s against the pattern's 26.01 s — a ratio of 2.16×,
   up from 1.36× at 10 MB. **This is where raising the threshold does help**: forcing the
   broadcast recovers 35.03 s, 1.35×.
2. **The pattern's margin over a correctly-configured relational plan is stable**, 1.31×
   at 10 MB and 1.35× at 100 MB. It is the *default* configuration that degrades with size,
   not the best available one.
3. **Broadcast cost grows with the dimension**: 4.8 s at 12 MB, 13.1 s at 100 MB, for the
   same query. The pattern's Phase 1 grew comparably, 3.2 s to 9.2 s.
4. **At 1 GB, neither the pattern nor a broadcast plan works.** Both fail on the same driver
   gather, at the same byte count, for the same reason. Only shuffle-based plans complete.

## 5. Results checked against the manuscript

Every claim in this section was verified against `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex` with the line
cited. One claim made in an earlier version of this report did not survive that check and
is withdrawn in §5.1.

### 5.1 Q2 — CORRECTED: this does not contradict the paper

An earlier version of this section claimed that Q2 contradicted the manuscript. **That claim
was wrong and is withdrawn.** The manuscript already publishes this result.

Checked against `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex`:

- **Line 1638–1639** — the per-query table gives the Joinless Partition Pattern at 13.40 s
  on Q2 and Spark Broadcast Hash Join at 8.04 s. The manuscript therefore already reports
  the pattern as 1.67× *slower* than BHJ on Q2.
- **Lines 200 and 1566** — "against the Broadcast Hash Join it ranges from $1.36\times$
  faster to $1.67\times$ slower, with the sign decided [by the workload]".

The measurement here — relational 12.21 s against the pattern's 17.37 s, a ratio of 0.70×,
i.e. the pattern 1.43× slower — falls inside the range the manuscript declares, and on the
same side for the same query. It **confirms** the paper rather than contradicting it.

What the measurement adds is the mechanism and the threshold dependency, neither of which
the manuscript records: the advantage appears only once the threshold admits `customer`
(`admits-3` gives 0.96×, `admits-4` gives 0.70×), and Q2 is the query whose two selective
dimension predicates reduce the join's build sides while the pattern still probes every fact
row.

### 5.2 The claimed applicability range is not reachable by the implementation

Checked against `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex`:

- **Line 835** — the applicability domain is "tables in the 10MB--1GB range, high
  aggregation ratio, equijoin".
- **Line 2089** — "**Medium-sized dimensions**: each dimension in the 10MB--1GB range".
- **Lines 844 and 2124** place the boundary at 1 GB, recommending hybrid approaches only
  for dimensions *above* it.

The dimension measured here is 998.20 MiB on disk — inside the declared range, just under
its upper bound. At that size,
Phase 1 does not complete: `buildIntArray` calls `collect()` on the whole dimension, and
254.7M rows exceed both `spark.driver.maxResultSize` and, when that is raised, a 32 GB
driver heap.

Two qualifications, both of which matter for how this should be reported:

- **This refutes the implementation, not the pattern.** Phase 1 builds an array of
  1,018,984,616 bytes — about 1 GB, comfortably resident in a 32 GB driver. It is the
  collection of 254.7M boxed `Row` objects on the way there that fails, not the structure.
  Building the array by partition-local aggregation rather than by `collect()` would avoid
  this, and the pattern as described in the paper does not require a driver-side gather.
- **Spark's own broadcast fails identically at the same scale.** The `admits-4` and
  `above-all-8x` configurations fail with the same error at the same byte count. So this is
  not a disadvantage of the pattern relative to the baseline; it is a limit that both
  broadcast-based strategies share, and the honest statement is that the upper end of the
  motivating range is unreachable for broadcast-based execution on this driver
  configuration, whichever of the two is used.

### 5.3 The reviewer's threshold objection, resolved

The objection was that raising `autoBroadcastJoinThreshold` would let Spark broadcast the
dimensions and leave the pattern unnecessary. What the measurements show:

- At the paper's scale, **the threshold does not need raising** — the largest dimension
  estimates at 7.51 MiB against a 10 MiB default (§2), and the default plan already carries
  `BHJ=4, SMJ=0`.
- With everything broadcast, the pattern is still faster on Q1 (1.36×), Q3 (1.14×) and Q4
  (1.49×), and slower on Q2 (0.70×).
- So the objection is **correct in substance for Q2 and incorrect for Q1, Q3 and Q4**, and
  the paper's failure to disclose the setting was material: a reader could not have told
  which of these applied.

Four defects found and fixed, each of which would have corrupted a result:

1. **`summarize.py` silently discarded the base variant.** A record was opened only on a
   `query =` line, and `JoinlessBase` emitted `variant =` without one. Its fields were folded
   into the preceding record — corrupting that baseline as well — and its five runs collapsed
   into one that was never emitted. This is why the optimization-path row was not regenerable.
2. **`RunReport` counted zero joins in every plan.** The runners `cache()` before the first
   action, so the executed plan is an `InMemoryTableScan` with no children and the joins are
   invisible to a normal traversal. Every configuration reported `BHJ=0`, indistinguishable
   from a plan that genuinely has none.
3. **Plan files were overwritten across sweeps.** Run labels are built from query and
   threshold, which collide between the main and medium sweeps. Eight files from the main
   sweep were overwritten with plans taken over a different dataset before this was caught;
   they were regenerated, and `JPP_PLAN_DIR` now separates them.
4. **`dimension_sizes*.csv` was malformed.** Ratio columns were formatted with the host
   locale, which uses a comma as the decimal separator, splitting the field and shifting
   every column after it. Now formatted with `Locale.ROOT`.

## 8. Outside the scope of the four tasks, but affecting reproducibility

**The committed generator does not reproduce the dataset the numbers come from.**
`datagen/GenerateSSB.scala` carries a header saying so, and two independent checks confirm it:

- `~/ssb_synth/lineorder` has a column `lo_orderkey` that the generator does not emit.
- The generator's `amount = (id % 10000) + 1` implies an aggregate sum of exactly
  3,000,300,000,000. The measured sum is 3,000,078,095,504.6, which is also not an integer.

Every table in the paper rests on a dataset that cannot be regenerated from this repository.
This is a more serious reproducibility problem than any single discrepancy in a results
table, and it is not addressed by this work.

**The repository README carries a superseded measurement generation.** It reports 175 groups
where the data gives 200, and an optimization path of 68.10 / 20.80 / 9.41 s where the
manuscript gives 89.24 / 14.51 s. Details and recommendation in
`reports/discrepancia_optpath.md`. The README was not rewritten, because correcting it
properly requires re-measuring DuckDB, ClickHouse, the two join baselines and the MinIO rows,
and replacing only some rows would produce a mixed table that is worse than the current one.

## 9. The generator (Task A)

Full detail in `reports/generador.md`. Summary, because it reverses §8:

**The dataset is reconstructible, and the reconstruction is verified.** §8 recorded that no
table in the paper could be regenerated from the repository. That was correct about
`datagen/GenerateSSB.scala` and wrong as a general conclusion.

The fact table's keys and `amount` are random draws, so reproducing it needs the RNG seed.
The seed was recovered rather than guessed: the table is written as 2000 contiguous
partitions, so row 0 is the first draw of partition 0, and Spark seeds `rand(seed)` per
partition, which makes the first draw a function of the seed alone and therefore searchable.

| Column | Rule |
|---|---|
| `lo_orderkey` | `id` |
| `lo_custkey` | `(rand(1) * 3000000).cast("long")` |
| `lo_suppkey` | `(rand(2) * 200000).cast("long")` |
| `lo_partkey` | `(rand(3) * 1400000).cast("long")` |
| `lo_orderdate` | `(rand(4) * 2556).cast("long")` |
| `amount` | `rand(5) * 10000.0` |

over `spark.range(0, 600000000, 1, 2000)`. Seeds are consecutive from 1, one per column.

Verified by seven checksums over all 600,000,000 rows, all matching, including
`sum_amount_bits` = 2,796,171,100,739,202,574,311,426,188 — the sum of the IEEE-754 bit
patterns, which is exact and order-independent and agrees only if every value is identical
bit for bit.

```bash
./scripts/jpp SeedSearch search 239.06964275028918 0 20000000 0 10000
./scripts/jpp VerifyGenerator ~/ssb_synth
```

The partition count is part of the rule, not a tuning detail: `rand` is seeded per
partition, so generating the same range with a different partition count — or inserting the
`repartition` that `GenerateSSB.scala` performs — changes every value in the table.

supported that claim**, because every benchmark ran one query per JVM. It is now measured.

./scripts/run_session_reuse.sh ~/ssb_synth

| **Joinless Partition Pattern** | **54.53** | 4.63 | **0** |

## 12. Sparse keys (Task C)

The 0.97× ratio of §4.1 holds only for dense keys. The array is sized by the **largest**
surrogate key, not by the row count, and dense keys are the precondition the manuscript
states for this optimization. A reviewer asked in the first round whether the structure can
exceed the raw data. For dense keys it does not; this is the other case.

```bash
./scripts/jpp GenerateSparseDims ~/ssb_sparse 10,100,500,700,1000 ~/ssb_synth/lineorder
./scripts/run_sparse_keys.sh ~/ssb_sparse
```

Row count, schema and attribute values are held fixed; only the key range varies. The rows
the fact table probes keep their keys and attributes, so every query returns the same 200
groups and the same sum at every spread factor.

| Spread | Rows | On disk (B) | Lookup structure (B) | Lookup / disk | Status |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 10× | 3,000,001 | 12,349,527 | 120,000,024 | **9.72×** | measured |
| 100× | 3,000,001 | 12,349,527 | 1,200,000,024 | **97.17×** | measured |
| 500× | 3,000,001 | 12,349,527 | 6,000,000,024 | **485.85×** | measured |
| 700× | 3,000,001 | 12,349,527 | 8,400,000,024 | **680.19×** | measured |
| 1000× | 3,000,001 | 12,349,527 | 12,000,000,016 | 971.70× | **fails** |

**Yes, and by a wide margin.** At a spread of only 10× the structure is already 9.7× the raw
Parquet. At 700× it is 680× — 8.4 GB resident in the driver and in every executor for a
dimension occupying 12 MB on disk.

Query time degrades far less than those ratios suggest:

| Spread | collect (s) | streaming (s) | Phase 1, collect (s) | Scan (s) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10× | 19.70 | 20.67 | 3.70 | 16.01 |
| 100× | 21.82 | 23.50 | 5.39 | 16.64 |
| 500× | 28.52 | 34.53 | 11.41 | 16.83 |
| 700× | 29.91 | 39.30 | 13.45 | 16.52 |
| 1000× | **fail** | **fail** | — | — |

The scan holds at roughly 16 s throughout: probe cost does not depend on the size of the
array. What grows is Phase 1, from 3.70 s to 13.45 s — the cost of allocating and filling a
structure that is mostly empty.

### Where the array stops being viable

The boundary is hard and is an addressing limit, not a memory one. The array is indexed by
an `Int`, so it cannot represent a key at or above `Int.MaxValue` = 2,147,483,647. For a
dimension of 3,000,000 rows that is a spread factor of **715.8**. Measured: 700× completes,
1000× fails in both Phase 1 variants. Beyond that point the `HashMap` variant is the only
option, which is what the manuscript's optimization-path table describes as the base case.

### A silent-corruption defect, found and fixed

The published `buildIntArray` reads the key through `getKeyAsInt`, which **truncates** a
`Long` to an `Int`. A key of 2,999,999,999 truncates to a negative value, which the loop's
`if (k >= 0)` guard then skips. The result was an array sized by whatever largest key
happened to fit, with the out-of-range rows dropped — no exception, no warning, wrong
answers.

Both `buildIntArray` and `buildFlagArray` now read the key as a `Long` and fail explicitly:

```
largest surrogate key in c_custkey is 2999999999, beyond the Int range an indexed
array can address (2147483647). The HashMap variant applies.
```

This matters for the paper beyond the defect itself: **the manuscript states dense keys as
the precondition of the indexed-array optimization, but there is a second, independent
precondition** — the largest key must fit in an `Int`. A dimension with perfectly dense keys
violates it as soon as it exceeds 2.1×10⁹ rows.

## 13. Phase 1 without a driver-side collect (Task E)

§5.2 recorded that the pattern cannot reach the top of its own declared applicability range
because Phase 1 collects the entire dimension to the driver. That diagnosis was correct and
the limit is removable.

`buildIntArrayStreaming` fills the same array from `toLocalIterator`, one partition at a
time, so peak driver memory is the array plus a single partition rather than the array plus
the whole dimension. Selected with `JPP_PHASE1=streaming`; the kernels, the scan and the
aggregation are the same code in both paths, so any difference is Phase 1 and nothing else.

```bash
./scripts/run_phase1_variants.sh ~/ssb_medium
```

| Scale | Variant | Elapsed (s) | Phase 1 (s) | Scan (s) | Groups |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 MB | collect | 20.72 | 3.62 | 17.09 | 200 |
| 10 MB | streaming | 20.79 | 4.79 | 15.95 | 200 |
| 100 MB | collect | 27.16 | 10.12 | 16.94 | 200 |
| 100 MB | streaming | 27.41 | 11.86 | 15.55 | 200 |
| 1 GB | collect | **fail ×5** (`maxResultSize`) | — | — | — |
| 1 GB | streaming | **90.89** | 74.61 | 15.90 | 200 |

Three things follow:

1. **The 1 GB point now completes**, with the correct group count and sum, where the
   published implementation fails in all five repetitions. The limit was an artefact of how
   Phase 1 was written, not a property of the pattern.
2. **The fix costs almost nothing where `collect` already worked**: +0.3% at 10 MB and +0.9%
   at 100 MB, within run-to-run variation.
3. **The scan is flat at ~16 s across all three scales**, which is the pattern's central
   structural claim holding under a 100× change in dimension size.

But completing is not winning. At 1 GB the pattern costs 90.89 s against the relational
plan's 68.48 s (§4.2) — **1.33× slower** — and Phase 1 is 74.61 s of that, 82% of the total
and 4.7× the scan it enables. `toLocalIterator` trades memory for latency: it runs one job
per partition, sequentially.

So §5.2 should be restated rather than withdrawn: the pattern can be made to cover its
declared range, but at the top of that range it is slower than the relational plan it is
proposed to replace, and the cost is concentrated entirely in structure building.

Note also that `streaming` is the wrong choice for the sparse case (§12): there `collect` is
faster at every spread factor, because the dimension has few rows and the cost is allocating
the array, not transferring the rows. The two variants address different failure modes —
streaming fixes *too many rows*, and neither fixes *too wide a key range*.

## 16. Two guards, and why they are failures rather than warnings

Partitioning is part of the experiment in two distinct ways, and getting either wrong
produces numbers that look correct.

**In generation.** `rand` is seeded per partition, so `spark.range(0, 6e8, 1, 2000)` at any
other partition count produces a *different table* with the same schema and the same row
count. A dataset regenerated at the environment's default parallelism is silently not the
published one.

**In execution.** The manuscript reports 250 Spark partitions on read over the 2000 files.
Two configurations compared while reading at different parallelism measure the parallelism,
not the pattern.

A warning is not enough for either, because a warning in a log nobody reads at the time is
how a wrong number reaches a table. Both guards invalidate the run.

### Guard 1 — `PartitionGuard`

Every run emits the partition count of the fact table and of each dimension, and throws if
any pinned value differs from what `JPP_EXPECT_PARTITIONS` declares.

```
partitions.lineorder        =    250  (expected 250, OK)
partitions.customer         =     16  (expected 16, OK)
```

Validated in both directions — a guard that never fires is not a guard:

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| `lineorder=250` (true value) | passes |
| `lineorder=999` | throws `IllegalStateException`, run invalidated |

It also caught a genuine error of the author's during §18: the scenario table is *written*
as 64 files but *read* as 16 partitions, because Spark packs small files into tasks. Write
partitioning and read parallelism are different quantities and were conflated; the run was
invalidated rather than reported.

### Guard 2 — `DatasetFingerprint`

Run before every sweep. Seven quantities over the fact table, compared exactly:

| Quantity | Published value |
|---|---:|
| rows | 600,000,000 |
| `sum_orderkey` | 179,999,999,700,000,000 |
| `sum_custkey` | 900,022,254,009,281 |
| `sum_suppkey` | 60,001,169,805,653 |
| `sum_partkey` | 420,010,238,860,186 |
| `sum_orderdate` | 766,518,981,463 |
| **`sum_amount_bits`** | **2,796,171,100,739,202,574,311,426,188** |

`sum_amount_bits` is the sum of IEEE-754 bit patterns: exact, order-independent, and equal
only if every one of the 600 million values matches bit for bit. The ordinary double sum is
deliberately excluded — it varies with the reduction tree, which varies with partitioning,
so it would report a difference between two identical tables. That error was made once
already (`reports/generador.md` §4).

The key columns are checked alongside it so that a dataset differing in the keys but
matching on `amount` is still caught.

Validated in both directions:

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| `~/ssb_synth` | 7/7 match |
| A deliberately different dataset (1M rows, 4 partitions) | 7/7 fail, run aborted |

`GenerateMediumDims` and `GenerateSparseDims` previously left dimension partitioning to the
environment; both now declare it (`DIM_PARTITIONS = 16`), pinned to the value the already
published measurements were taken at, so regenerating reproduces them rather than shifting
them.

## 17. Caching the dimensions (Task F)

§14 recorded that §10 measured a relational plan written the way a user would write it, and
that explicitly caching the dimensions might reduce the per-query broadcast. It was measured.

```bash
./scripts/run_session_reuse.sh ~/ssb_synth
```

Four regimes, Q1–Q4 in one session, five repetitions each, first discarded, interleaved.

| Regime | Session (s) | Setup (s) | Broadcast (ms) | BHJ | SMJ | Shuffle |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Joinless Partition Pattern** | **54.15** | 4.67 | **0** | 0 | 0 | 2 |
| Relational, default threshold | 74.88 | 0.00 | 16,334 | 4 | 0 | 2 |
| Relational, cached, default threshold | 253.25 | 3.61 | 1,622 | **2** | **2** | **6** |
| Relational, cached, 100 MiB threshold | 76.00 | 3.42 | 14,758 | 4 | 0 | 2 |

### The answer

The valid comparison is `relational` against `relational-cached-hi`, because those two carry
**the same plan**. With the plan held constant:

**Caching does not eliminate the per-query broadcast. It reduces it by 9.6%** — 16,334 ms to
14,758 ms — and the session total does not improve (74.88 s to 76.00 s).

A `BroadcastExchange` is dominated by building and shipping the relation, not by reading its
source. Caching saves the read, which is the small part. Separate queries still produce
separate exchange instances, so the relation is rebuilt either way.

The pattern's session advantage therefore stands without qualification: 54.15 s against
74.88 s for the best relational regime.

### A trap worth publishing

Cached at the *default* threshold, the session is 3.4× **worse**, not better. Caching
replaces the Parquet scan with an `InMemoryRelation`, whose size estimate is the in-memory
representation rather than the compressed bytes on disk:

| Dimension | Estimate, on disk (B) | Estimate, cached (B) | Inflation | Over 10 MiB? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| `customer` | 7,876,669 | 57,625,714 | 7.32× | **yes** |
| `part` | 2,512,572 | 11,200,000 | 4.46× | **yes** |
| `supplier` | 363,778 | 1,671,111 | 4.59× | no |
| `date` | 22,820 | 40,896 | 1.79× | no |

Two dimensions cross the default threshold, their broadcast joins become sort-merge joins,
and the fact table is redistributed twice more (shuffle 2 → 6).

**Caching the dimensions can silently degrade a relational plan**, with nothing in the log to
announce it, purely because a size estimate changed. Had only that regime been measured, the
honest-looking report would have been "caching reduces broadcast by 91%" — true, and
thoroughly misleading, because the reduction came from *losing two broadcasts*, not from
making them cheaper.

## 19. `.par` against a single combined query (Task H)

A reviewer objected that the paper collapses multiple scenarios into one execution using
Scala's `.par`, that this resembles multi-query optimization, that combining the queries into
one should have been tried and compared, and that as it stands it looks like a static
implementation that works only for the author's case.

### Two facts about scope, established before measuring

**`.par` is not in the current manuscript or in this repository.** An exhaustive search finds
no occurrence in any `*.scala`, in the git history, or in `joinless_v8_Jul.tex`,
`joinless_v8_Jul_30.tex`, `joinless_v9_Jul_31.tex` or `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex`. It appears
in versions v2–v5 (in `/home/athenas/Descargas/`), inside a *Case Study 3: Financial scenario
unpivoting (3 scenarios × 10M records)* that was removed.

**It is not multi-query optimization.** Per `joinless_v5.tex:889-910`, `scenarios.par.map`
inside `mapPartitions` expands each input row into one row per scenario, reading
`VALUATION_METHOD_SC_i`, `PROTECTION_METHOD_SC_i` and `COUNTERPARTY_EXPOSURE_SCi`. It is an
**unpivot**, parallelised with threads inside the partition.

The input table is therefore a **reconstruction** from that description — the original does
not exist on this host — and the measurement concerns material the paper no longer contains.

```bash
./scripts/jpp GenerateScenarios ~/ssb_scenarios
./scripts/run_multiquery.sh ~/ssb_scenarios
python3 scripts/summarize_multiquery.py results/multiquery.csv
```

### Equivalence

All three variants produce 30,000,000 output rows and agree exactly, per scenario:

| Scenario | Rows | Exposure | Distinct valuations | Distinct protections |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 10,000,000 | 4,999,613,857,747.23 | 4 | 4 |
| 2 | 10,000,000 | 4,999,719,024,866.01 | 4 | 4 |
| 3 | 10,000,000 | 4,999,587,133,569.77 | 4 | 4 |

Checked per scenario rather than in aggregate: an unpivot that attached rows to the wrong
scenario would still balance in total.

### Times

| Variant | Median (s) | vs `par` |
|---|---:|---:|
| `par` — the published implementation | 46.18 | 1.00× |
| `seq` — the same loop, `.par` removed | 12.85 | **3.59× faster** |
| `sql` — the single combined query | **8.69** | **5.31× faster** |

### What follows

1. **The reviewer is right that the single query is better.** It produces identical results
   and is 5.31× faster.
2. **`.par` is not merely unnecessary; it is a pessimization.** The same loop with the
   parallel collection removed is 3.59× faster. Constructing a parallel collection per row,
   over ten million rows, to distribute three elements, costs far more than it saves. This is
   why the `seq` variant exists: comparing only `par` against `sql` would have confounded the
   cost of the parallel collection with the cost of leaving Spark's plan, and would have
   attributed all of it to the latter.
3. **The plans show why `sql` wins.** `par` leaves a `Scan ExistingRDD` — the unpivot happens
   outside the plan, and Spark sees an opaque RDD of boxed `Row`s, with no column pruning, no
   pushdown and no code generation. `sql` shows
   `FileScan parquet → Generate explode(array(struct(...))) → Project`: the whole
   transformation is in the plan, over columnar Parquet.
4. **On "static implementation":** `par` and `seq` read their columns by interpolated name
   inside the row loop, so the scenario set is fixed in the shape of the code — a different
   set is a different program. `sql` builds its array of structs from a list, so a different
   set is a different argument. The reviewer's characterisation is accurate, and the single
   query does not share the limitation.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10× | 12,349,527 | 12,456,424 | 9.72× | 9.63× | −0.9% |
| 100× | 12,349,527 | 13,526,670 | 97.17× | 88.71× | −8.7% |
| 500× | 12,349,527 | 15,251,255 | 485.85× | 393.41× | −19.0% |
| 700× | 12,349,527 | 15,324,996 | 680.19× | 548.12× | −19.4% |
| 1000× | 12,349,527 | 15,373,555 | 971.70× | 780.56× | −19.7% |

**The upper bounds of §12 were tight.** The ratios fall by at most 19.7%, and the conclusion
is unchanged: at a spread of 10× the structure is still 9.6× the raw Parquet, and at 700× it
is 548×.

The on-disk size saturates near 15.3 MB, +24.5% over the dense case. Past a certain
irregularity delta encoding stops helping and the full key width is paid, so the compression
penalty has a ceiling — which is why the ratio drop stalls around 19% rather than continuing.

The `Int` bound fires again at 1000×, with a largest key of 2,999,999,325 — a different value
from the tip construction because of the jitter, and the same conclusion.

### What is not measured here, and why

The scattered construction moves the keys the fact table references, so most fact rows no
longer find a dimension row and the queries do not return the published answer. Sizes remain
fully comparable, since they do not depend on running a query, but **no timings are reported
for this mode**. Preserving the answer would require holding keys 0…2,999,999 dense, which is
exactly what leaves no room to scatter at a fixed row count. The tension is inherent to the
question, not an artefact of the measurement, and the timings of §12 remain the ones to use.

## 23. Third round: defects found in this work

1. **The partition guard caught the author conflating write partitioning with read
   parallelism** (§16). The scenario table is written as 64 files and read as 16 partitions;
   the run was invalidated rather than reported.
2. **A completion watcher fired early**, because its marker matched both the inner and outer
   sweep scripts. It was corrected to a marker unique to the outer script. No measurement was
   affected — the sweep continued correctly — but the status reported from it was wrong until
   checked against the process itself.
3. **Measuring only the obvious form of the caching objection would have produced a true and
   thoroughly misleading number** (§17): "caching reduces broadcast by 91%", when the
   reduction came from losing two broadcasts to sort-merge joins and the session was 3.4×
   slower. A fourth regime with the threshold raised was needed to compare like with like.
4. **Comparing only `par` against `sql` would have attributed the whole 5.31× to leaving
   Spark's plan** (§19). The `seq` variant separates the two effects and shows that 3.59× of
   it is the parallel collection alone.

**At a 1 GB dimension the pattern is slower than the best available relational plan on every

### 24.4 What this changes about §20

§20 concluded that the divergence between queries was governed by predicate selectivity. That
remains correct **at 10 MB and 100 MB**, where Q2 is the only query below 1.00× and Q3 —
which has a predicate, but a weak one — tracks Q1 and Q4.

At 1 GB a second effect takes over that does not discriminate by query: Phase 1 costs 70–98 s
whatever the query is, while the scan stays at 13–17 s. Selectivity still sets the *spread*
between queries (Q2 at 0.23× against Q1 at 0.79×), but it no longer sets the *sign* — every
query is below 1.00×.

Q4 makes the mechanism concrete. It has no predicate on `customer`, so a broadcast plan would
have to ship the whole gigabyte, and `admits-4` and `above-all-8x` **fail with
`maxResultSize`** — as they do for Q1 and Q3. The relational plan cannot broadcast, falls back
to `BHJ=1, SMJ=1`, and **redistributing a 1 GB dimension across the network still costs less
(56.64 s) than building the same dimension into an array on the driver (82.94 s)**.

Only Q2 can still broadcast the dimension at 1 GB, because its region predicate cuts the build
side to about a fifth — and that is exactly why its ratio is the most extreme of the four.

### 24.5 Against the manuscript

Checked against `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex`, lines cited:

- **`:835`** — the applicability domain is "tables in the 10MB--1GB range, high aggregation
  ratio, equijoin". **`:2089`** repeats it. A 1 GB dimension is inside the declared domain,
  at its upper bound.
- **`:200` and `:1566`** — "against Broadcast Hash Join it ranges from $1.36\times$ faster to
  $1.67\times$ slower, **with the sign decided by predicate selectivity**".

Two findings, and they point in opposite directions:

**The mechanism the manuscript names is confirmed.** §20 arrived at predicate selectivity
independently, from Q1–Q4 across two scales, before this section was written. The manuscript
is right about *why* the sign moves.

**The bound it states does not hold at the top of its own declared domain.** The only
configuration at 1 GB that is a pure Broadcast Hash Join is Q2's `admits-4` (`BHJ=4, SMJ=0`)
at 31.13 s, against the pattern's 108.59 s — **3.49× slower**, where the manuscript's stated
worst case is 1.67× slower. For Q1, Q3 and Q4 a pure BHJ is not available at all at 1 GB: the
broadcast fails, which is itself outside what the stated range contemplates.

The distinction matters and is not rhetorical. The range at `:200` describes the paper's own
query set at its own scale — `customer` at 3M rows, about 12 MB — and is accurate there.
It is not literally a guarantee across the whole domain. But `:835` declares 10MB–1GB as the
domain of applicability, so a reader would reasonably carry the bound to the top of it, and
there it is exceeded by a factor of 2.1.

## 26. Where the pattern stops winning (crossover)

§25 named the interval between 100 MB and 1 GB as the most valuable gap: the sign flips
somewhere inside it, and neither endpoint tells a reader where. Two intermediate sizes were
generated and all four queries measured at each.

```bash
./scripts/jpp GenerateMediumDims ~/ssb_medium 300,600 ~/ssb_synth/lineorder
SWEEP_ONLY="pattern default-10MiB admits-3 admits-4 above-all-8x" \
JPP_PHASE1=streaming SIZES="dim300mb" QUERIES="q1 q2 q3 q4" \
  ./scripts/run_threshold_sweep_medium.sh ~/ssb_medium ~/ssb_synth
```

200 points. Both guards ran before each sweep, `JPP_PHASE1=streaming` throughout — with
`collect` the large points fail and the sweep loses its `joinless` reference, which is what
every ratio in the table is computed against. Equivalence held in all eight new combinations.

`admits-1` and `admits-2` were not run: they were never the best relational configuration at
any measured scale, so dropping them cannot change which configuration the "best relational"
column reports. The sweep logs what it drops.

### Best relational plan ÷ pattern

| Query | 10 MB | 100 MB | 300 MB | 600 MB | 1 GB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Q1 | 1.31 | 1.35 | 1.24 | **1.07** | 0.79 |
| Q2 | 0.71 | 0.53 | 0.46 | 0.31 | 0.23 |
| Q3 | 1.17 | **1.15** | 0.92 | 0.68 | 0.54 |
| Q4 | 1.47 | 1.32 | **1.28** | 0.92 | 0.68 |

(Below 1.00 the relational plan is faster. Bold marks the last measured point at which the
pattern still wins.)

### The crossover

Bracketed by measurement, with the point inside each bracket obtained by linear interpolation
and **labelled as an interpolation, not a measurement**:

| Query | Bracketed between | Interpolated crossover | Dimension predicates |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Q2 | below 1.00 at every size measured | **< 10 MB** | two at 1/5 each |
| Q3 | 100 MB (1.15) and 300 MB (0.92) | ~230 MB | one at 2/5 |
| Q4 | 300 MB (1.28) and 600 MB (0.92) | ~530 MB | none, 2 dimensions |
| Q1 | 600 MB (1.07) and 1 GB (0.79) | ~700 MB | none, 4 dimensions |

**The crossover falls in four different places, spanning at least a 70× range.** A single
number would have been wrong for three of the four queries: taking Q1's ~700 MB and applying
it to Q3 overstates the pattern's range by roughly 3×, and Q2 has no crossover inside the
declared domain at all — it is already behind at the smallest size measured.

### What orders them

The ordering is not arbitrary and follows two effects that were each identified separately
earlier in this report:

1. **Predicate selectivity first** (§20, and the mechanism the manuscript names at
   `joinless_v10_Jul_31.tex:200`). Q2's two 1/5 predicates shrink the join's build sides by
   about 25× while the pattern still probes every fact row and, worse, builds a second array
   for the filtered dimension (§24.1). It crosses before the range even begins. Q3's single
   2/5 predicate produces a much weaker version of the same effect, and it crosses at ~230 MB.
2. **Number of dimensions second**, among the queries with no selective predicate. Q1 joins
   four dimensions and Q4 two, so the relational baseline has twice as much redistribution to
   pay for in Q1 — which keeps the pattern ahead longer, to ~700 MB against Q4's ~530 MB.

So the pattern's advantage is bounded from two directions at once, and the bound that binds
first depends on the query.

### For the paper

The manuscript declares 10MB–1GB as the applicability domain (`:835`, `:2089`). These
measurements say that within that domain the pattern wins on the whole range only for a query
with four dimensions and no selective predicate, and loses across most of it for a query with
two selective predicates. A domain stated as a size range alone does not capture that; the
crossover depends on the query at least as strongly as on the dimension size.
